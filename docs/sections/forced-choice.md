# Forced choice

**In one line:** you see a small set of statements about how people behave and pick the one most like you and the one least like you.

Talogy says the bulk of the Caliper Profile's questions use this format. There are no right answers. What matters is that your choices describe you accurately, because they feed every trait score on your report.

## What it measures

Forced-choice items measure personality traits, the motivations and natural tendencies Caliper scores. Talogy explains that no single set produces a score. Instead, "by combining all of the individual's responses," it scores you on each trait. A trait score is built from the pattern of many choices across the whole assessment.

That is why one awkward set does not decide anything, and why answering steadily and honestly matters more than agonising over any one choice.

## How the questions look

Talogy describes the format like this: you are given a set of behavioural statements and asked to choose which one best describes you and which one least describes you.

In practice that means:

- Every statement is about everyday behaviour or preference, written at a reading level Talogy puts at US grade 6 to 8.
- The statements in a set can all sound reasonable. Often two feel true and none feels clearly false.
- You make two choices per set: one "most like me" and one "least like me". The others are left unmarked.

The worked examples below use the same shape, with original statements written for these notes.

## A method that works

1. **Read all the statements before choosing.** The "most" choice is relative to the others in the set, so you need to see them all.
2. **Picture a normal working week.** Not your best day, not your worst. Ask which statement you would recognise yourself in most often.
3. **Pick "most like me" first.** Go with the one that is true most often and most strongly.
4. **Then pick "least like me".** Look for the statement that is clearly the opposite of how you behave. If none is opposite, choose the one that fits least, even if the gap is small.
5. **Do not mark for the job.** Choose what is true of you, not what you think the employer wants. The reasons are in [why faking backfires](#why-faking-backfires).
6. **Move on.** Spend seconds, not minutes, on each set. First honest reactions are usually the most accurate.

## Traps that cost accuracy

- **Answering as your ideal self.** "I would like to be someone who loves public speaking" is not the same as being one. Describe how you are now.
- **Answering for a role.** If you are applying for a sales job, statements about persuading people can look like the "right" picks. They are only right if they are true of you.
- **Treating "least" as "never".** The least-like statement does not have to be something you never do. It only has to fit you less than the others.
- **Overthinking one set.** A set where nothing fits is normal. Pick the closest and the furthest, then keep going. Your scores come from the whole pattern.
- **Changing your frame halfway.** If you describe "me at work" for the first half and "me at weekends" for the second, your answers will contradict each other. Keep one frame: you at work, on a typical week.

## Why faking backfires

Talogy says the Caliper Profile is carefully designed to resist deliberate manipulation of trait and job-fit scores, for three reasons it names:

1. **Forced choice.** Research, Talogy says, shows forced-choice items reduce fakability. You cannot mark every flattering statement as top; you have to choose between them.
2. **Length.** Keeping up a false picture across 98 questions is hard, and inconsistencies build up.
3. **Local norms.** Scores are compared with people in your country of employment, so you are not judged against an imagined ideal.

There is also a plainer reason. The job-fit score compares you with what predicts success in a particular role. If you describe someone you are not, a good fit score only means you may be hired into a job that suits that imaginary person. Honest answers are the ones that protect you.

## Timing

The profile is untimed, and Caliper says the whole assessment takes about an hour. Forced-choice sets are short to read. A steady pace of a few seconds of reading and one honest decision per set keeps you fresh for the reasoning section.

## Practice items

These 10 worked examples are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from the Caliper Profile. On the real test there is no right answer: the right answer is whatever is true of you. Here, each item describes a person, and the answer is what is true of **that person**, using only the description. Items 7 to 10 check that you understand how the format works.

For items 1 to 6, choose the statement **most** like the person and the one **least** like them.

**Item 1.**

> Maya plans her week every Sunday evening and keeps a running checklist. She gets uneasy when a meeting starts with no agenda.

A) I like having a clear plan before I start.
B) I enjoy helping colleagues with their problems.
C) I would rather make it up as I go.
D) I like working outdoors.

**Item 2.**

> Daniel sells software. He loves the moment a doubtful customer comes round to his view. When a prospect says no, he shrugs it off and makes the next call.

A) Winning someone over to my view gives me a real buzz.
B) I take rejection personally and dwell on it.
C) I like to read instructions in full.
D) I enjoy cooking for friends.

**Item 3.**

> Priya is a nurse. She notices when a patient is anxious even before they say anything, and colleagues often come to her when something is wrong.

A) I can usually tell how someone feels before they say it.
B) I often miss it when someone is upset.
C) I like fixing machines.
D) I prefer to work at night.

**Item 4.**

> When his manager changes the plan in the middle of the week, Sam adapts without fuss. He finds strict step-by-step procedures frustrating and likes trying new approaches.

A) I adjust easily when plans change.
B) I like every task to follow a fixed procedure.
C) I enjoy public speaking.
D) I keep a tidy desk.

**Item 5.**

> Ana likes working with others, but what she is known for is accuracy: she checks every figure in a report twice before sending it.

A) I check my work carefully for small errors.
B) I enjoy working with other people.
C) I tend to skip the fine print.
D) I like learning languages.

**Item 6.**

> Leo stays calm in a crisis and is the person others look to when things go wrong. He is not especially chatty, but he does not mind small talk.

A) I stay composed when things go wrong.
B) I get flustered when things go wrong.
C) I love chatting with strangers.
D) I like puzzles.

**Item 7.** In a set, none of the four statements describes you well. What should you do?
A) Skip the set.
B) Pick the one that fits best and the one that fits least, even if the differences are small.
C) Pick at random to save time.
D) Pick the statements an employer would most like to see.

**Item 8.** Which frame gives the most accurate profile?
A) You as you think the job wants you to be.
B) You at work on a typical week.
C) You as you hope to be in five years.
D) You as your best friend would describe you on holiday.

**Item 9.** Which reasons does Talogy give for the Caliper Profile's resistance to faking?
A) Forced-choice items, the length of the assessment, and norms based on local culture.
B) A strict time limit and a live proctor.
C) Checking answers against your CV.
D) Right and wrong answers on every personality item.

**Item 10.** Talogy says trait scores come from combining all of your responses. What does that mean for a single set?
A) Each set decides one trait score on its own.
B) Scores come from patterns across many sets, so one awkward set carries little weight.
C) Only the first ten sets count.
D) Sets where you hesitated are thrown away.

<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 10</summary>

**Item 1: Most A, least C.** Maya plans every week and dislikes meetings without an agenda, so A fits and C ("make it up as I go") is its opposite. B and D are not mentioned, so they are neither.

**Item 2: Most A, least B.** Daniel enjoys bringing customers round, which is A. He shrugs off rejection, which is the opposite of B.

**Item 3: Most A, least B.** Priya reads people's feelings before they speak, which is A. B is the opposite.

**Item 4: Most A, least B.** Sam adapts to changed plans (A) and finds fixed procedures frustrating, the opposite of B.

**Item 5: Most A, least C.** Both A and B are true of Ana, but the description says accuracy is what she is known for, so A is the stronger fit. Skipping the fine print (C) is the opposite of checking every figure twice. This is the typical forced-choice moment: two true statements, and you pick the one that is more true.

**Item 6: Most A, least B.** Staying calm in a crisis is A, and getting flustered (B) is its direct opposite. *Why not C as least:* Leo is not chatty, but he does not mind small talk, so C fits him less well but is not contradicted. B is the clearer mismatch.

**Item 7: B.** The format asks which statement describes you best and which least, so the choice is relative. Small differences are fine. *Why not D:* choosing for the employer gives a false picture, which is exactly what the format is built to resist.

**Item 8: B.** The profile measures your real tendencies at work. A typical week is the most accurate, consistent frame. *Why not A or C:* those describe an imagined person, not you.

**Item 9: A.** Talogy names the forced-choice format, the length of the assessment and the norming of scores to local culture. *Why not B:* the profile is untimed and Caliper says no proctor is required. *Why not D:* only the abstract reasoning items have right answers.

**Item 10: B.** Because trait scores combine responses from across the assessment, no single set decides anything. That is a good reason to answer each set quickly and honestly rather than agonising.

| Answer | A, C | A, B | A, B | A, B | A, C | A, B | B | B | A | B |

For items 1 to 6 the first letter is "most like", the second "least like". Count an item as right only if both are right.

- **9 to 10:** you understand the format. On the real test, use the same habit on yourself: most true first, clearest opposite second.
- **6 to 8:** look at items 5 and 6. Choosing between two true statements, or two untrue ones, is the skill that matters most.
- **5 or fewer:** reread the method and the traps, then redo the items. The key idea is that "most" and "least" are relative to the other statements in the set.

These are worked examples, not a personality score. On the real Caliper Profile there are no right answers to the personality items.
